Why 4 in 10 construction apprentices don't finish — and what helps

Published 17 November 2026 · Figures checked 17 November 2026 · 4 min read

Roughly 4 in 10 trade apprentices drop out within four years. That's a waste for everyone: the apprentice, the employer who trained them, and a housing industry that badly needs qualified tradies. The good news is that the main reasons people leave are things employers and families can do something about.

What the numbers say

The National Centre for Vocational Education Research (NCVER) tracks every apprenticeship contract in Australia. Its latest completion report, released in mid-2026, was reported as showing:

  • about 42% of trade apprentices who started in 2021 had dropped out within four years;†
  • given more time, 58.7% of trade apprentices who started in 2019 had finished within six years;†
  • electrical apprentices had the highest six-year completion rate, at about 72%.†

The stakes are high. BuildSkills Australia's 2025 housing workforce study estimated the country needs an extra 116,700 workers to build the 1.2 million homes targeted under the National Housing Accord by 2029.† The Housing Industry Association has called for more apprentices and stronger training support alongside targeted skilled migration to close the gap. Every apprentice who finishes is one more qualified tradie.

† We couldn't open the NCVER and BuildSkills reports ourselves (their sites block automated checking), so these figures come from published summaries of them. Links are below.

Why apprentices leave

When NCVER surveys people who didn't finish, the most common reasons are about the job rather than the training. In the 2025 survey, 27.7% of trade non-completers said their main reason was dissatisfaction with pay, conditions or the workplace.† Other common themes in the research are:

  • Money pressure in the first two years. A first-year junior apprentice on the building award earns $654.49 a week before tax. That's tight if you're running a car or living out of home.
  • Relationship with the boss or crew. Being the junior on site can be tough, and poor supervision or a bad culture pushes people out.
  • Not enough variety or training on the job. Apprentices who feel like cheap labour rather than trainees lose motivation.
  • The business runs out of work. Small builders' workloads go up and down, and an apprentice can be the first to go.
  • Finding out the trade isn't for them. Some of this is unavoidable — and leaving isn't always a bad outcome. In NCVER's survey, 77% of apprentices and trainees who left early were employed afterwards.

What helps: for employers

  • Talk about pay early and plan the steps. Show the apprentice how their rate goes up each year and when the Key Apprenticeship Program payments are due. If you can pay above award in the first year, it's one of the strongest retention tools you have.
  • Use the incentives to fund support. The KAP employer incentive (up to $5,000 in the first year) can pay for a good set of tools, extra supervision time or a modest pay bump.
  • Give them a mentor who isn't their boss. A senior tradie who checks in weekly makes a big difference, especially in year one.
  • Plan the learning. Map out which skills they'll learn in which quarter and tick them off together. It keeps the work from feeling repetitive.
  • Use your Apprentice Connect provider. They offer free in-training support and can step in early if things go wrong.
  • Consider a Group Training Organisation if your workload is lumpy. A GTO can move the apprentice to another host when you're quiet, so their training keeps going.

What helps: for apprentices and parents

  • Know your numbers. Work out your real take-home pay, including the year-by-year increases and KAP payments, before you commit to a car loan or a rental.
  • Check you're being paid correctly. Underpayment is a common reason people feel undervalued. The Fair Work Pay Calculator is free.
  • Speak up early. If something's wrong at work, talk to your Apprentice Connect provider before you quit. They can mediate or help you transfer to another employer and keep your progress.
  • Try before you sign. A pre-apprenticeship course or work experience helps you find out whether you like the work before you're locked in.
  • Look after your head. Construction can be a hard environment. Mates in Construction and other industry programs offer free, confidential support.

Frequently asked questions

What's the completion rate for electrical apprentices?
Electrical apprentices had the highest six-year completion rate among the trades in NCVER's latest data, at about 72%, according to published summaries of the report.
Can I change employers without starting my apprenticeship again?
Usually, yes. Your Apprentice Connect provider can help transfer your training contract so the time you've already done counts.
Do employers lose incentives if an apprentice leaves?
Incentive instalments are paid at set points (for example, 6 and 12 months) and the apprentice must still be in training at that point, so an early departure can mean a missed payment.
Is dropping out a disaster?
Not necessarily. Most people who leave an apprenticeship are working afterwards. But finishing gives you a licence or qualification that's hard to get any other way.
